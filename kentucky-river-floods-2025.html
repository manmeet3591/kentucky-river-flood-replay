What satellites saw of the 2025 Kentucky River floods

We replayed the April and February 2025 floods with NASA's OPERA satellite water maps and the USGS gauges at Locks 4, 12 and 14 and on the North Fork at Jackson. The replay shows what satellites caught, what they missed, and what a multi-satellite system could add for lock-and-dam inspections and short-range outlooks.

Kentucky River at Lock 4, Frankfort · USGS 03287500

April 2025: the crest at Frankfort

The river at Lock 4 crested at 48.35 ft on April 7, the second-highest stage on record. Sentinel-1 radar imaged the reach twice: once just before the crest, and again a week later.

February 2025: one flood wave through Locks 14 and 12

The crest passed Jackson on the North Fork on February 16, Lock 14 at Heidelberg (below the forks) 11 hours later, and Lock 12 about 20 hours after that. A single Sentinel-1 pass on the evening of February 17 imaged only part of the basin.

In development

Filling the gaps between looks

The April flood again, drawn as observations over time. In April 2025, Sentinel-1 looked at this reach twice in two weeks. The system we are developing is designed to update the flood map every six hours during an event and to issue 0–72 hour outlooks from the latest map.

Satellites

More looks at the river

NISAR L-band radar (launched July 2025) sees water under tree cover. It joins Sentinel-1 and commercial X-band radar from NASA's commercial data program, harmonized onto one water scale.

Between passes

Maps between looks

An AI model held to river physics (National Water Model flows, terrain, USGS gauges and Kentucky Mesonet rainfall) carries the flood forward between satellite passes.

Products

Depth, duration and recession

Where the water is, how deep it is, how long it stays and how fast it leaves each reach, on a 30 m grid.

Ahead

0–72 hour outlook

Corrected inundation outlooks at 6, 24, 48 and 72 hours, scored against the National Water Model and persistence.

Trust

Confidence on every pixel

Each map carries an uncertainty range and the age of its most recent satellite observation, so you know how fresh it is.

Delivery

Works in existing GIS

Standard map services that load into existing GIS tools. A public-data version runs without any commercial license.

Decisions the maps are meant to support

Who it could help

Lock and dam operators, emergency managers, water utilities and floodplain managers make different decisions on different clocks. These are examples.

Damage assessment

Lock-and-dam inspections

Flood extent, depth and recession timing by reach, so inspection crews can follow the water down the river and reach each structure as soon as it is safe.

Preparedness

Planning before crests

A 0–72 hour outlook along the corridor to anticipate impacts at locks, water intakes and riverside communities.

Records

A record of every flood

Extent, depth and duration for each event, to document how floods move through the basin and where they recur.

Two-way

Field observations sharpen the maps

Readings and photos from lock sites and field crews during a flood can check and correct the satellite maps in near real time.

What's next

Next steps

More floods

Longer replays

Replay more Kentucky River floods and compare the satellite maps with records from lock sites and field crews.

NISAR

Seeing under the trees

Add NISAR L-band radar, which can detect flood water under forest canopy, as its calibrated data record grows.

Depth

How deep, not just where

Estimate flood depth by combining the water maps with Kentucky's lidar elevation data and the lock gauges.

This is a research replay of past floods, not a forecast or warning. For current river conditions and forecasts, use the National Weather Service (water.noaa.gov) and the USGS (waterdata.usgs.gov).
